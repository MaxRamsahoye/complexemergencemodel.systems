  2. Daniel Schmachtenberger

    The Emergence Model

    Schmachtenberger describes a society as three interacting layers: its infrastructure, the technologies and material systems through which it meets its needs; its social structure, the institutions of politics, law and economics; and its superstructure, its shared values, beliefs and culture. A change in any layer pressures the others, but the deepest and most durable shifts begin in the infrastructure. The model gives this project its way of tracing how a new technology stack reshapes political economy and cultural psychology.

    • Ken Wilber

      Integral Theory

      Wilber’s four-quadrant map holds that any whole has interior and exterior, individual and collective dimensions – experience, behaviour, culture and systems – and that none can be reduced to the others. It underlies the Emergence Model’s insistence that technological, institutional and cultural change be read together.

      Source ↗

    • Marvin Harris

      Cultural Materialism: The Struggle for a Science of Culture

      1979

      Harris divides every society into infrastructure, structure and superstructure, and argues that the material conditions of production and reproduction are the primary drivers of cultural change. It is the source of the Emergence Model’s three layers, and of the priority it gives to infrastructure.

      Source ↗

  3. Karl Marx

    Preface to A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy

    1859

    In a few pages Marx sets out the materialist conception of history: the relations of production make up the economic structure of society, “the real foundation, on which arises a legal and political superstructure and to which correspond definite forms of social consciousness”. The base-superstructure model is the first of the layered models of civilisation this project traces.

    Source ↗

  4. Complex Systems & Emergence

  5. P. W. Anderson

    More Is Different

    Science, 1972

    Anderson argues against the idea that knowing the fundamental laws means understanding everything built from them: at each level of complexity entirely new properties appear, and the science of each level is as fundamental as any other. A founding statement of emergence.

    Source ↗

  6. Herbert A. Simon

    The Architecture of Complexity

    Proceedings of the American Philosophical Society, 1962

    Simon observes that complex systems, from organisms to organisations, tend to be hierarchic – built of nested subsystems – and “nearly decomposable”, with strong interactions within each subsystem and weaker ones between them. Such structures evolve far faster than unstructured ones, because stable intermediate forms can be assembled step by step.

    Source ↗

  7. Donella H. Meadows

    Thinking in Systems: A Primer

    2008

    Meadows introduces the basic elements of systems – stocks, flows and feedback loops – and shows how a system’s structure produces its own behaviour. Her account of leverage points, the places to intervene in a system, ranks changes to its goals and paradigms above changes to its parameters.

    Source ↗

  8. Joseph A. Tainter

    The Collapse of Complex Societies

    1988

    Tainter treats societies as problem-solving organisations that meet each new challenge by adding complexity – more institutions, specialists and control. Complexity has costs and its returns diminish; past a point it leaves a society more vulnerable rather than less, and collapse becomes an economical simplification.

    Source ↗

  9. Structure, Agency & Conditioning

  10. B. F. Skinner

    Science and Human Behavior

    1953

    Skinner extends the experimental analysis of behaviour to human life: behaviour is shaped by its consequences, as actions followed by reinforcement become more likely. He applies operant conditioning to government, religion, economics and education – the institutions through which a society conditions its members.

    Source ↗

  11. Pierre Bourdieu

    Outline of a Theory of Practice

    1977

    Bourdieu’s habitus is a system of durable dispositions, instilled by the conditions in which a person grows up, that generates practices adapted to those conditions without conscious calculation. Structures are internalised and reproduced in action – and, through action, remade.

    Source ↗

  12. Anthony Giddens

    The Constitution of Society: Outline of the Theory of Structuration

    1984

    Giddens refuses the choice between structure and agency. Social structures are both the medium and the outcome of the practices they organise – the “duality of structure” – so that agents reproduce, and can transform, the conditions that enable and constrain them.

    Source ↗

  13. Existential Risk

  14. Nick Bostrom

    Existential Risk Prevention as Global Priority

    Global Policy, 2013

    Bostrom defines an existential risk as one that threatens the premature extinction of Earth-originating intelligent life, or the permanent and drastic destruction of its potential for desirable future development, and argues that reducing such risks deserves far greater priority given how much of the future is at stake.

    Source ↗

  15. Toby Ord

    The Precipice: Existential Risk and the Future of Humanity

    2020

    Ord surveys the risks to humanity’s long-term potential, natural and anthropogenic, and puts the chance of existential catastrophe this century at about one in six – most of it from risks of our own making, with unaligned artificial intelligence the largest.

    Source ↗

  16. Luke Kemp, Chi Xu, Joanna Depledge, Kristie L. Ebi, Goodwin Gibbins, Timothy A. Kohler, Johan Rockström, Marten Scheffer, Hans Joachim Schellnhuber, Will Steffen & Timothy M. Lenton

    Climate Endgame: Exploring Catastrophic Climate Change Scenarios

    Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 2022

    The authors argue that the worst outcomes of climate change are dangerously under-studied, and set out a research agenda for them – including how warming could cascade through food systems, conflict and politics to trigger global societal collapse or even human extinction.

    Source ↗
