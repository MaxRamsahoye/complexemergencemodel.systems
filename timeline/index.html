Timeline

The intellectual genealogy of The Complex Emergence Model.

    Five models of how a civilisation is structured, from the Complex Emergence Model back to Marx’s base and superstructure. Each divides a society into interacting layers – material, institutional and cultural – and each builds on, or answers, those before it.

      1. 2026

        Complex Emergence Model

        Max Ramsahoye, The Complex Emergence Model (2026)

        An elaboration of Schmachtenberger’s framework of civilisational structures that expands the number of structures to eleven: four core structures, two interfaces with ‘second nature’, a foundation in universal physics, two that transect the rest, and the two scales of human agency, individual and collective. Every structure is at once exo – an extended phenotype or prosthesis, built outward from human agency – and endo – internalised, shaping that agency from within.

        Infrastructure · Tech Stack
        Technological instruments and affordances, productive (and reproductive) forces and material conditions.
        Interstructure · Political Economy
        The institutional complex for decision-making (governance) and resource-allocation (economy).
        Superstructure · Cultural Psychology
        Ideologies, worldviews, belief and value-systems, symbols, memetics and narratives.
        Substructure · Evolutionary Psychology
        Universal, innate cognitive-affective predispositions and capacities.
        Biostructure · Cultural-Evolutionary Biology
        The physiological, genetic, somatic and neurobiological substrate of human populations.
        Ecostructure · Cultural-Evolutionary Ecology
        The biophysical environment, resource reservoirs, ecosystem dynamics and planetary boundaries.
        Metastructure · System Dynamics
        The cybernetic, game-theoretic and evolutionary mechanisms operational within a civilisation.
        Macrostructure · Developmental Process
        The process by which a civilisation elaborates itself and changes across macrohistorical time.

      2. 2017

        Emergence Model

        Daniel Schmachtenberger, ‘The Emergence Model’, The Emergence Project (2017)

        Schmachtenberger describes a society as three interacting layers: its infrastructure, social structure and superstructure. A change in any layer pressures the others, but the deepest and most durable shifts begin in the infrastructure.

        Infrastructure
        The technologies and material systems through which a society meets its needs.
        Social Structure
        The institutions of politics, law and economics.
        Superstructure
        The shared values, beliefs and culture.

      3. 1995

        Integral Model: Four Quadrants

        Ken Wilber, Sex, Ecology, Spirituality: The Spirit of Evolution (1995)

        Wilber’s four-quadrant map holds that any whole has interior and exterior, individual and collective dimensions – experience, behaviour, culture and systems – and that none can be reduced to the others. It underlies the Emergence Model’s insistence that technological, institutional and cultural change be read together.

        Upper Left · I
        The individual interior: intention, experience and consciousness.
        Upper Right · It
        The individual exterior: the body, brain and observable behaviour.
        Lower Left · We
        The collective interior: shared culture, meaning and worldview.
        Lower Right · Its
        The collective exterior: social systems, institutions, technology and the environment.

      4. 1979

        Cultural Materialism: Three Structures

        Marvin Harris, Cultural Materialism: The Struggle for a Science of Culture (1979)

        Harris divides every society into infrastructure, structure and superstructure, and argues that the material conditions of production and reproduction are the primary drivers of cultural change. It is the source of the Emergence Model’s three layers, and of the priority it gives to infrastructure.

        Infrastructure
        The modes of production and reproduction: technology, subsistence, demography and the environment a society works within.
        Structure
        The domestic and political economy: kinship, the household, the division of labour, classes and the state.
        Superstructure
        The symbolic and ideational: art, ritual, religion, science and the values a society holds.

      5. 1859

        Classical Marxism: Base-Superstructure

        Karl Marx, A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy (1859)

        Marx divides a society into an economic base – its forces and relations of production – and a legal, political and ideological superstructure that rises on it. The base conditions the superstructure: “It is not the consciousness of men that determines their being, but their social being that determines their consciousness.”

        Base
        The forces of production – tools, techniques, labour – and the relations of production: who owns, who works, and how the product is shared.
        Superstructure
        Law, politics, religion, art and philosophy: the institutions and forms of consciousness that rise on the economic base and work to sustain it.
